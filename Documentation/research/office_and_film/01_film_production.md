## Furniture sourcing & eras

**Who.** Production designer Danny Vermette; art director Alan Derksen; set decorator Trevor Johnston ([S13](https://filmandfurniture.com/2026/06/backrooms-the-furniture-film-of-the-year/), [S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)). TMDB lists Elliott Vion (set decoration + coordinator), Chloe Bohonos (set dec coordinator), property master **Dana Bontempo**, concept artist Brett Macdonald, graphic designers Brendan Megannety / Hazel Owen / Wenke Li, construction coordinators Adam Bartlett / Chris Thompson, chief lighting technician Yannie Yu ([S22](https://www.themoviedb.org/movie/1083381-backrooms/cast)). Lead/set-dec buyer **Eric Cairns** (Galerie snippet, S4; prior TMDB extraction). The same Vermette / Derksen / Johnston team had done Osgood Perkins' *The Monkey* (S1, per prior search snippet — UNVERIFIED).

**Era rule.** Johnston's constraint was that nothing could post-date the early 1990s ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)). Vermette describes the period look as mass-produced, catalog furniture: "everything was so mass produced. It just felt like a Sears catalog", with "94 pages of couches" ([S4](https://galeriemagazine.com/how-horror-hit-backrooms-terrorizes-viewers-with-design/)).

**Where it came from (verified).**
- Facebook Marketplace listings and estate sales across British Columbia; Johnston's team went wherever there was "a gold mine of untouched '90s furniture" ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)).
- Estate sales, clearance spots, hotel liquidators and Facebook Marketplace ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)).
- **Hotel liquidator sale = the duplicate engine.** One sale gave Johnston **20 identical, well-preserved upholstered sofas** and **40+ identical oak slat-back dining chairs** ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)). Surface reports **39 of the same chair from one haul**, reused "as oddities, barriers, and the bones of nonsensical creations" ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)). (39 vs "more than 40" — same haul, different counts; treat as ~40.)
- Vermette on duplicates: the volume of "this one patterned couch" pumped out in the '80s–'90s is visible on screen ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)).
- Some pieces were built in-house by Johnston's team (S1 mentions it but gives no construction details).

**Eras / styles / colours named in sources.**
- Clark's store (Cap'n Clark's Ottoman Empire): bulging La-Z-Boy recliners, maple bedroom and dining sets, '90s inflatable furniture, hand-painted EVERYTHING MUST GO sign ([S7 Dwell](https://www.dwell.com/article/backrooms-a24-production-design-5cc5d402); the inflatable-furniture line is Dwell's era context, not a confirmed set piece); boxy TVs playing infomercials, display lamps around a floor-model bed, NO CREDIT NEEDED signage ([S8 AD](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/inside-backrooms-set-where-even-205429099.html)).
- Colour progression in the store: sad greys and blues upstairs → neutral beige → the yellow world ([S6 W](https://www.wmagazine.com/culture/backrooms-a24-movie-set-design-details-interview)); W also quotes Vermette that finding mint-condition '90s pieces that could plausibly be for sale in a store was hard, and that Kane's childhood references had interesting fabrics and patterns in neutral tones.
- Backrooms set dressing: wooden ladder-back dining chairs; cheap moulded plastic seating; orange motel doors with bright blue chairs and yellow tables; thin pine spindle balustrades; a gold throne ([S13](https://filmandfurniture.com/2026/06/backrooms-the-furniture-film-of-the-year/)). The throne and "real furniture" from Clark's store were deliberately pulled into the backrooms ([S5 IndieWire](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/backrooms-production-design-ultimate-game-200000184.html)) so the audience sees that the place "is getting its influence from somewhere".
- Mary's spaces: "greige therapy office" ([S3](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/meaning-behind-surreal-sets-a24-195154619.html)); Mary's couch was built up as a deliberate '90s storytelling piece ([S6](https://www.wmagazine.com/culture/backrooms-a24-movie-set-design-details-interview)); therapist's office uses floral chintz, wood panelling and dated '90s tech ([S8](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/inside-backrooms-set-where-even-205429099.html)). A Galerie search snippet says lead buyer Eric Cairns scoured Facebook Marketplace for mint-condition finds for Clark's showroom, Mary's office and home, and select corners of the Backrooms ([S4](https://galeriemagazine.com/how-horror-hit-backrooms-terrorizes-viewers-with-design/), snippet; the fetch did not surface the line).

**Brands.** Only La-Z-Boy (recliners, S7, verified) and Sears (as a *catalog reference*, S4) are named. No office-furniture brand (Steelcase, Herman Miller, Haworth) is named in any source found. => Treat office brands as UNKNOWN.

## Piles: construction & intent

### What the piles are made of (verified)
- **Furniture Pile Room** = the first space after the Null Zone/basement wall. Inventory listed by Film and Furniture: restaurant chairs, televisions, cabinets, sofas, shelving, dressers, lamps, hatstands ([S13](https://filmandfurniture.com/2026/06/backrooms-the-furniture-film-of-the-year/)).
- **Chairs as the base unit.** Wooden ladder-back dining chairs either clip through floors (low-res game-glitch language) or are stacked and abandoned in oversized rooms ([S13](https://filmandfurniture.com/2026/06/backrooms-the-furniture-film-of-the-year/)). The ~40 identical oak slat-back chairs from the hotel liquidator are the obvious source ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview), [S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)).
- **Escalation:** the furniture "begins as merely drab" and culminates in "ceiling-sweeping piles of Frankensteined ottomans and chairs" ([S3 Elle Decor](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/meaning-behind-surreal-sets-a24-195154619.html)). => Piles grow toward the ceiling deeper in the film; Still B (~2.5 m, nearly touching the ceiling) is a late/deep example.
- Reviewers: "a wasteland of cheap furniture, some melding with the floor or walls, some piled up like obstacle courses or a bonfire-in-waiting" ([S15 BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/backrooms-kane-parsons-turns-internet-mythology-into-unsettling-inventive-horror)); "rooms with couches and furniture buried in the midst of floors and walls", "shoes standing upright embedded in the carpet" ([S14 Moria](https://moriareviews.com/sciencefiction/backrooms-2026.htm)); Dwell: furniture exists only "to be amassed and arranged in delicate piles" ([S7](https://www.dwell.com/article/backrooms-a24-production-design-5cc5d402)). Note "delicate": the piles read as balanced arrangements, not wreckage.
- Pieces "part-consumed by the carpet, the wall, or the ceiling" ([S6 W](https://www.wmagazine.com/culture/backrooms-a24-movie-set-design-details-interview)).

### Physical construction — what is and is not known
- **Inspiration = game clipping.** Vermette drew the furniture idea from digital "clipping", objects in video games appearing spliced at odd angles ([S3](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/meaning-behind-surreal-sets-a24-195154619.html)); Johnston frames it as "no-clipping", items pushed through walls and solid objects ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)).
- **Practical first.** Vermette: "We really wanted to have as much practical as we could" ([S3](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/meaning-behind-surreal-sets-a24-195154619.html)).
- **Duplicates were real duplicates** — bought in bulk (20 sofas, ~40 chairs) rather than modelled, so repeated objects on screen are physically identical units ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)). The 39 chairs became "the bones of nonsensical creations" ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)) — i.e. the identical chairs are the skeleton/armature of the sculptural piles.
- **Digital distortion pass.** Pieces Johnston sourced or built were **3D-scanned so VFX could digitally distort them** ([S1](https://thesetset.com/articles/backrooms-set-decorator-trevor-johnston-interview)). So some interpenetration/warping is VFX on top of scanned real furniture; the article does not say which shots.
- **VFX scale & method context:** 830 shots, 600 with VFX, 1 h 18 min of VFX incl. 10 min full CG; wall-clipping was practical-first (actors walk through real openings, walls restored in post), and supervisor Edward Douglas says the real trick of clipping is the shadows ([S21](https://vfxvoice.com/where-practical-ends-and-vfx-begins-in-backrooms/)). Mr. Wolf VFX handled set extensions and clipping ([S21](https://vfxvoice.com/where-practical-ends-and-vfx-begins-in-backrooms/)). => Contact shadows / AO at the intersection line are what sell an object passing through a surface.
- **NOT FOUND anywhere:** whether piles were bolted, screwed, glued, welded or cut-and-joined; whether pieces were sawn to fake embedding in floors/walls. Industry-standard practice (cut the piece at the floor/wall plane and fix it to the set; screw stacked pieces together for safety) is plausible but **UNVERIFIED** for this film. The Blu-ray "Prop Walkthrough with Kane Parsons" (1:30) and "Building the Backrooms" (26 min) are the likeliest primary sources ([S23](https://shop.a24films.com/products/backrooms-blu-ray); the shop page lists titles and runtimes only).

### Story intent
- Vermette/Parsons deliberately pulled real furniture from Clark's store, plus his throne, into the backrooms so the audience reads that the place takes its influence from somewhere ([S5](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/backrooms-production-design-ultimate-game-200000184.html)).
- **The mechanism stated in dialogue:** Clark says the place builds the rooms, or rather "remembers" them, and that the more times it remembers something, the less it does ([S17 Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Backrooms); same line in [S16](https://taylorholmes.com/2026/06/03/the-backrooms-movie-what-actually-happens-and-ending-explained/)). He also compares the copies (Still Lifes) to furniture — they simply exist "like furniture" ([S17](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Backrooms)). He likens the place to describing a dog to someone who has never seen one and asking them to draw it ([S17](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Backrooms)). => **Design rule: a pile is a degraded re-drawing of familiar furniture, not damage.** Each repetition of the same object should be slightly *less* right (scale, leg count, proportion, mirrored text), never broken/splintered.
- **First pile = proof object.** In Clark's first visit there is a pile of furniture in the centre of the room; on the way out he grabs a chair as proof ([S16](https://taylorholmes.com/2026/06/03/the-backrooms-movie-what-actually-happens-and-ending-explained/)). Later he passes half-formed shop signs and furniture clipping through walls; another room has shoes half-clipped through the floor and a hole stuffed with discarded chairs ([S16](https://taylorholmes.com/2026/06/03/the-backrooms-movie-what-actually-happens-and-ending-explained/)). The claim that the chair is "two chairs melded into one" grown by the backrooms appears in the prior run's search snippets but was NOT found on the re-fetched Taylor Holmes page => UNVERIFIED.
- **Pile reading:** Rolling Stone reads the first pile as jumbled and haphazard, suggesting "a makeshift sanctuary or an arcane, sacred ritual" ([S26](https://au.rollingstone.com/?p=95956)). AP: "art installation versions of office space", with piles of furniture and shrunken doors ([S27](https://wtop.com/entertainment/2026/05/movie-review-backrooms-goes-from-internet-meme-to-the-big-screen/)). A search-engine summary attributes "a pile of furniture with too many legs" to a review (UNVERIFIED — source page not located).
- **Doorway / blue tape:** the boundary between the showroom and the world beyond is later marked out with blue tape ([S13](https://filmandfurniture.com/2026/06/backrooms-the-furniture-film-of-the-year/)). This matches Red's Still A (unfinished doorway outlined in blue painter's tape, exposed studs) — i.e. Still A is very likely the Furniture Pile Room next to the doorway back to the store. Prior-run fandom trivia (five-legged stool in front of the pile next to that doorway) is UNVERIFIED (fandom pages return 402).
- **Copies are slightly wrong, and get less wrong over time:** early signs in the Backrooms are mirror-image; Clark says the space remembers things slightly wrong; later copies become more legible but still off (e.g. a fast-food sign in Cyrillic) ([S24 Looper](https://www.looper.com/2183614/backrooms-movie-small-details/)). Behind the showroom wall Clark first finds merchandise piled up as if disgruntled employees had dragged it there ([S24](https://www.looper.com/2183614/backrooms-movie-small-details/)) — i.e. the first pile is meant to read as *plausible* clutter before it reads as impossible.
- **Repetition with drift (Everything Must Go short, shown with the film's second run / on the Blu-ray):** three identical store-closing signs in a row, one slightly embedded in the floor, the others hanging from the ceiling; researchers cut through a wall and find a partial replica of the store ([S31](https://noscoopsclub.com/2026/07/03/decoding-backrooms-everything-must-go/)). A fandom summary adds that the preceding signs shrink in scale and sink further into the floor (UNVERIFIED — fandom 402).
- **Landmarks:** towers of furniture (and caveman cut-outs playing the Voyager Golden Record) act as signposts for characters and viewers ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)). => Piles are navigation anchors, which is directly useful for a maze game.
- **Reading by critics (interpretive, not production fact):** Clark's showroom of beds nobody sleeps in and sofas nobody sits on becomes the template the Backrooms copies without understanding ([S30 Enicola](https://paulenicola.substack.com/p/review-backrooms-makes-bad-furniture): staged rooms with the shape of home and none of the life; chairs stacked into impossible configurations). The Astromech's reading that the space copies the store without understanding it (https://www.theastromech.com/2026/05/backrooms-plot-explained-every-major.html) is UNVERIFIED (prior), not re-fetched.
- **"Nothing is broken":** no source uses this phrase. It is consistent with (a) bulk-bought identical, well-preserved pieces (S1), (b) "delicate piles" (S7), (c) the "remembers… less" rule (S17). Treat as a well-supported design inference, not a quote.

## Office spaces

What the film's offices are, per verified sources:
- Sony: sets included "a wide open office space with random furniture", a single-person square tunnel and a pool; no removable walls, few entry points, strange hallways and ill-fitting doors ([S11](https://sony-cinematography.com/dp-jeremy-cox-and-venice-2-ground-the-extradimensional-reality-of-backrooms/)).
- Moria: "endless regular offices but empty of people where dividers go nowhere; wall cabinets that extend the entire length of a hall"; rooms of low-hanging chandeliers ([S14](https://moriareviews.com/sciencefiction/backrooms-2026.htm)).
- AP: the chambers resemble vacant, nondescript office spaces, but as "art installation versions of office space" ([S27](https://wtop.com/entertainment/2026/05/movie-review-backrooms-goes-from-internet-meme-to-the-big-screen/)).
- BFI: "endless partitions, walls, stairs, doors and ceilings"; dead tech such as floppy discs and cassette tapes ([S15](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/backrooms-kane-parsons-turns-internet-mythology-into-unsettling-inventive-horror)).
- Dwell frames the decade as fluorescent-studded popcorn drop ceilings, seas of cubicles and overstuffed or inflatable furniture, and reads the backrooms through Koolhaas's "Junkspace" ([S7](https://www.dwell.com/article/backrooms-a24-production-design-5cc5d402)). This is the critic's cultural framing; it does NOT confirm that cubicle farms were built.
- Ceiling: 2'x4' drop-ceiling grid with 180 fixtures ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)); Vermette notes the "big expanses of dropped ceiling" had to be supported by infrastructure above ([S9 The Credits](https://www.motionpictures.org/2026/06/how-production-designer-danny-vermette-made-backrooms-real-portals-platforms-practical-terror/)). Parsons calls drop ceilings a symbol of uniformity and efficiency ([S18 A24 Notes](https://a24films.com/notes/2026/05/thirty-thousand-square-feet-with-kane-parsons-james-wan)); he also recalls that in the original photo it was unclear whether it was an office space or the back of a furniture store, and that his Blender sets are "pretty much one for one" with the film ([S18](https://a24films.com/notes/2026/05/thirty-thousand-square-feet-with-kane-parsons-james-wan)).
- Floor: the backrooms are carpeted throughout (27,000 sq ft per S6/W; 29,000 per the Wikipedia film page — UNVERIFIED (prior)). **No source mentions carpet *tiles*, cubicle panel systems, CRT monitors on desks, or specific office-furniture brands in the film's Backrooms sets.** CRT-type "boxy TVs" are confirmed only in Clark's store ([S8](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/inside-backrooms-set-where-even-205429099.html)); dated '90s tech in the therapist's office ([S8](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/inside-backrooms-set-where-even-205429099.html)).
- Async facility (real-world, 1990/91): had to be "100 percent era-correct" ([S9](https://www.motionpictures.org/2026/06/how-production-designer-danny-vermette-made-backrooms-real-portals-platforms-practical-terror/)), drawing on Parsons' childhood photographs and neighbourhood; Threshold Chamber was practical door + floor only, rest CG by CoSA VFX ([S21 VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/where-practical-ends-and-vfx-begins-in-backrooms/)).

**Bottom line for Red's office target:** the film's offices are *emptied* offices (dividers, partitions, wall cabinets, drop ceilings) with stray furniture, not populated 1990s cubicle farms. Red's target image (beige CRTs, blue-grey fabric panels, laminate desks, copier, water cooler, vending machine) is closer to a generic early-'90s office reference (and possibly to Kane Pixels' Async interiors — UNVERIFIED, fandom pages unreachable) than to anything documented in the film — which is fine, but it should be labelled as FrontRooms' own Office level rather than "film-accurate".

## Lighting/camera treatment of furniture

**Lighting is the ceiling, and nothing else (Backrooms interiors).**
- Cox: in the Backrooms scenes they did not place a single light; the tubes live in the ceiling fixtures ([S12 ShotDeck](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)).
- 180 fluorescent-style fixtures in a 2'x4' drop-ceiling grid designed in Blender by Cox and Parsons; each fixture holds 2 Astera Titan tubes (360 total) at 4000K, plus 50 Astera Helios tubes; all on a dimmer board with minimal mid-take changes ([S10 ASC](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)).
- Tube placement mattered: if tubes sit too close to the diffuser they read as film lights, so they hunted for a placement where you don't perceive the tubes ([S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)). => the lens/diffuser should glow evenly, not show tube hot-stripes.
- Light spacing and ceiling-tile spacing were stress-tested in Blender before build ([S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)).
- Explicitly avoided: shallow depth of field, traditional backlight, side key ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)). => furniture is never rim-lit or isolated; it receives the same flat top-light as the carpet.
- "Comparatively well-lit. Perhaps crushingly so" — horror from too much fluorescence, not darkness ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)).

**Colour.**
- Fixtures 4000K, camera WB 4300K (a 400K warm shift); a 3000K shift was rejected because skin went with it, so the wallpaper itself was printed more yellow ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/), [S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)).
- Company 3 colourist Joseph Bicknell built a LUT adding "another 20 percent shift" in a controlled way, avoiding cartoon yellow ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)).
- Wallpaper went through ~40–50 camera tests ([S8](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/movies/articles/inside-backrooms-set-where-even-205429099.html) says 40 product tests); the carpet had to avoid moiré, have the right tonality and pile, and be paintable/ageable ([S4](https://galeriemagazine.com/how-horror-hit-backrooms-terrorizes-viewers-with-design/)).

**Grain / halation / film.** The feature was shot digitally (Sony Venice 2). ASC mentions **no grain, halation or film emulation**; the only analog treatment is printing found-footage sections to VHS tape after grade/VFX ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)). Red's description of the stills as "shot on film (grain, halation)" is therefore a *look* reading, not a production fact. Cox: they did not want the film to feel glossy or "inherently cinematic" ([S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)). A search snippet claims Cox applied a haze that feels like watching on a CRT (UNVERIFIED — not in the Sony or ShotDeck text re-fetched).

**Lenses / camera.** Sony Venice 2 in Rialto mode (chosen because it mimics placing a camera node in Blender); Zeiss Supreme Primes 15–85 mm; Red Komodo (Super-16 crop) + Canon 8–64 mm for handheld POV ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)). **Inside the backrooms everything is 18 mm or wider; outside 25/28 mm and up; inside, "a 14mm looks normal"** ([S11 Sony](https://sony-cinematography.com/dp-jeremy-cox-and-venice-2-ground-the-extradimensional-reality-of-backrooms/)). Exposure T/2.8–T/4 ([S11](https://sony-cinematography.com/dp-jeremy-cox-and-venice-2-ground-the-extradimensional-reality-of-backrooms/)) => deep focus; furniture far away stays sharp.

**Composition rules.** Cox broke his own flat, zero-tilt, perpendicular-to-back-wall habit: off-centre subjects, slight short-siding, asymmetry; because the space is so uniform, an "obscure tilt" still holds together ([S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)). Handheld POV should feel accidental but hit the beats ([S10](https://theasc.com/article/backrooms-cinematography-cox/)). References: Reddit liminal-space photos and *One Hour Photo* ([S12](https://community.shotdeck.com/articles/an-interview-with-backrooms-cinematographer-jeremy-cox/)). Sightlines are cut by pony walls or sharp corners ([S2](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/a24-backrooms-production-design/)). Camera height: no source gives a number (UNVERIFIED); the POV/handheld material implies eye level.

Key shift: the photo is empty; the series introduced **stretched / phased** furniture (a digital, Blender-native distortion); the film translates that into **duplicated real objects** — multiplicity and interpenetration rather than stretching — because it is practical-first. For FrontRooms, the film's version (identical real-scale pieces, recombined) is cheaper and more convincing in a real-time PBR pipeline than stretched meshes.

## Implications for the game (concrete, numbered)

Context: `FrontRoomsFurniturePile.Build(parent, localCenter, radius, ceilingHeight, seed)` and `FrontRoomsOfficeKit.Dress(parent, localFloorXZ, ceilingHeight, seed, keepClear)`; rooms are recycled, zone heights 2.4 / 2.9 / 5.4 m, Mac laptop GPU.

**A. Pile content and grammar (from S1, S2, S3, S7, S13, S17)**
1. **Few meshes, many identical instances.** The film's piles are built on bulk-bought *identical* units (≈40 oak slat-back chairs, 20 identical sofas). Mirror that: a pile library of ~12–14 hero meshes, where one chair mesh supplies 30–45% of all pile instances and one sofa mesh appears 1–3 times. Identical repetition *is* the anomaly ("too many of the same object"); do not add per-instance colour or proportion variation to the repeated chair. All instances share a mesh + material (SRP Batcher / GPU instancing friendly), so recycled rooms cost no new memory.
2. **Pile palette = domestic/hotel furniture, not office furniture.** Documented pile items are restaurant/dining chairs, TVs, cabinets, sofas, shelving, dressers, lamps, hatstands (S13); store stock is recliners and maple bedroom/dining sets (S7). Red's stills add a Queen-Anne armchair, chest of drawers, hutch, glass display cabinet, club armchair, turned-leg side table on casters, bar stools, pleated lamp, halogen torchiere, CRT TV, steel filing cabinet, bookcase, pallet + crate. Recommended hero list (priority order): (1) oak ladder/slat-back dining chair, (2) floral 3-seat sofa, (3) chest of drawers, (4) wood hutch/cabinet, (5) Queen-Anne wing/armchair, (6) club armchair, (7) bookcase, (8) glass display cabinet, (9) CRT TV, (10) halogen torchiere + pleated table lamp, (11) bar stool, (12) side table on casters, (13) 4-drawer steel filing cabinet, (14) pallet + crate. Office pieces (filing cabinet, CRT) should be a minority accent in piles.
3. **Nothing is broken.** No source supports wreckage; pieces were "well-preserved" (S1) and piles are "delicate" (S7). Rule: no fractures, splinters, debris, scattered drawers or torn upholstery. Distortion comes only from transform (tilt 15–50°, upside-down, on-side), interpenetration, embedding, and rare scale/duplication errors. Wear = light dust on up-facing surfaces only.
4. **Degradation by repetition (S17, S24).** Drive a `drift` value from depth/sequence: drift 0 = plausible clutter (Looper: first pile reads like staff dumped stock), drift 1 = ceiling-sweeping (S3). Map: target pile height `H = lerp(0.45, 0.95, drift) * ceilingHeight` (on 2.9 m rooms 1.3 → 2.75 m; on 5.4 m zones cap at ~3.2 m so it stays readable); interpenetration depth `5% → 25%` of each piece's bbox; probability of a "wrong copy" (mirrored text decal, 0.85x/1.2x scale outlier, fused double chair) `0 → 0.3`.
5. **Cheap "too many legs".** Pre-bake 2–3 composite meshes in Blender headless: two copies of the hero chair boolean-unioned at a 3–8 cm offset (6–8 legs, doubled back slats), a sofa whose arm continues into a second sofa, a stool with five legs. These are static assets, instanced like any other piece. (The "fused chair" story beat and the five-legged stool are UNVERIFIED, but the composites are a low-cost, on-theme device.)
6. **Build order for `Build()`** (deterministic from `seed`): (a) base course 3–6 heavy carcasses (sofa, dresser, hutch, display cabinet, pallet) placed within `0.6*radius`, each rotated about a random horizontal axis 0–45°, one edge/corner sunk 3–20 cm into the floor; (b) middle course 4–10 chairs/armchairs/stools, upside-down or on-side, intersecting the course below by 5–25% of bbox; (c) crown: 1 sofa or armchair on top (Still B) plus one tall thin piece poking up (torchiere, lamp, hatstand, CRT); (d) 0–3 "escapees" at the pile rim: chairs sunk into the carpet up to the seat, shoes upright in the carpet (S14). Reject a placement if a piece neither touches the floor nor intersects another piece (no floating) or if its top exceeds `ceilingHeight - 0.08 m` (unless drift > 0.8, where one piece may enter the ceiling plenum — "part-consumed by … the ceiling", S6). 12–25 pieces total.
7. **Sell intersections with contact darkening, not effects.** VFX supervisor: clipping is sold by the shadows (S21). Keep SSAO on; add a baked/approximate contact-shadow blob decal where pieces meet the carpet and a soft AO band at mesh–mesh intersections (vertex-AO or a small decal), never sparkles/glitch shaders.
8. **Embedding into surfaces: prefer floor and ceiling.** Floor and ceiling sinks are free (geometry below the floor or above the tile plane is hidden). Wall embedding needs care in the streamed room train and maze, because the protruding part can appear in the neighbouring room: only embed into walls whose back is a solid/unused cell, or clip in the shader with a world-space plane, or keep wall embeds shallower than wall thickness.
9. **Collision:** one box/capsule collider per pile sized to the dense core (≈0.7 x radius), not per-piece mesh colliders; `keepClear` rects must keep the chase line open (as in the Codex layout). Piles are navigation **landmarks** (S2): at most one per room, placed off-axis so it is seen from the doorway.

**B. Office kit (from S11, S14, S27, S15, S18, plus Red's target)**
10. **Label honestly:** the film documents *emptied* offices (partitions, dividers that go nowhere, full-length wall cabinets, drop ceilings, "random furniture"), not populated cubicle farms, CRT desks, carpet tiles or named brands. Red's target (blue-grey fabric panels ~1.5 m, beige laminate desks on dark-brown frames, beige pedestals, beige 15" CRTs, 5-star black chairs, copier, water cooler, vending machine, 24" carpet tiles, 2'x2' mineral-fibre grid with water stains) is FrontRooms' own 1990s office — build it to that reference.
11. **Film-derived anomalies to add to `Dress()`:** (a) a partition run that continues past its desk island and dead-ends or enters a wall/column ("dividers go nowhere", S14); (b) one wall of identical cabinets/filing cabinets running the full length of a long wall (S14); (c) "too many of the same": 8–14 identical task chairs crowded into one cubicle, or every desk carrying the identical CRT angle; (d) mirrored text on 1 in N labels/signs (S24); (e) an office pile variant where office pieces are the minority accent inside a domestic pile (store stock leaking in, S5); (f) pony walls/partitions placed so sightlines stop short (S2).
12. **Partition and desk geometry should be beveled, worn and separately-materialed** (fabric panel with trim cap, laminate top with edge band, painted-steel frame, plastic CRT bezel) to escape Codex's boxy flat-colour look; the film's emphasis on mint, mass-produced pieces (S4, S6) means clean but slightly yellowed plastics and faded fabric — not grime.

**C. Light, camera and grade (from S10, S11, S12)**
13. **One light source family only.** Lights are the ceiling fixtures; no fill, no rim, no accent on furniture (S10, S12). Pile and office props must be lit by the same troffer grid as the floor. For the office use 2'x4' lay-in / surface troffers with **flat, evenly glowing lenses** (S12: tubes must not read through the diffuser) — the current Office lookdev frame's egg-crate/grid lens should be replaced by a flat frosted or fine-prismatic lens matching Red's target. Use dimmer-style intensity variation per fixture rather than flicker-heavy effects.
14. **Colour temperature:** film tubes 4000K with camera balanced at 4300K (slightly warm), yellow pushed into the wallpaper, then a controlled ~20% LUT push (S10). For the cooler Office target, keep fixture CCT ~4000K and shift the grade toward green-grey rather than recolouring lights.
15. **Lens:** film uses ≤18 mm full-frame inside the backrooms (14 mm "looks normal"; HFOV 90–104°), 25 mm+ outside (S11). Current game camera = 76° vertical FOV ≈ 108° horizontal at 16:9 ≈ 13 mm FF-equivalent, slightly wider than the film's widest. A range of 60–72° vertical (≈18–14 mm) matches the film. No shallow DoF (S10; T/2.8–T/4 on wide primes ⇒ everything sharp); keep chromatic aberration and barrel distortion minimal (the film wanted nothing glossy or "cinematic", S12).
16. **Grain/halation:** not documented for the feature (digital Venice 2; only found-footage was printed to VHS, S10). Keep the existing grain subtle as a stylistic choice; avoid strong halation/bloom on furniture highlights; reserve VHS-style degradation for any in-game "found footage"/camcorder moments.
17. **Composition for authored shots/screenshots:** avoid perfectly centred one-point perspective as a default; Cox deliberately framed off-centre with slight short-siding and tolerated tilt because the space is uniform (S12). Place piles off the corridor axis so the first view of them is asymmetric.

**D. Sourcing / pipeline**
18. **Assets:** nothing from the film is licensable; build or source generic CC0 analogues (Poly Haven, etc. — see the model-sourcing report) of the 14 hero pieces. Because identical repetition is the point, invest in quality per hero mesh (bevels, UV-mapped PBR, 2K textures) rather than in variety.
19. **Blue-tape threshold** (S13): an unfinished doorway with exposed studs outlined in blue painter's tape is a documented film motif and a strong "exit" signifier; if used, keep it generic (tape + studs), and avoid replicating store signage, pirate branding or the film's wallpaper.
